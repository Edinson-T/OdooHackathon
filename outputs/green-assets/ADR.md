# Design decisions

- Use hosted Google OAuth so credentials stay with the identity provider. New accounts default to community access.
- Treat a date-only inspection as due at local midnight. Open claims exactly 24 hours earlier. Unscheduled reports and environmental incidents can be investigated immediately, except in a zone with a flood warning.
- Serialize local mutations with Web Locks. Check existing assignments in the same transaction that claims a job.
- Keep field data local for this demo. Browser roles guide the UI; protecting shared records requires server-side authorization.
- Use configurable source URLs and explicit sensor units. The demo thresholds are 25 mm daily rain, a water level of 1.2 m for a watch and 1.5 m for a warning, and 15 and 20 mosquitoes per trap-day. Values must strictly exceed their thresholds. Every number sits in `src/domain/hazardConfig.ts`. These are illustrative thresholds for the demo and need expert review.
- Give alerts two levels. A watch says a threshold is about to be crossed: for rain, the 24 hour forecast is above the warning threshold. A warning says it is crossed now. A level ends only when the reading falls below 80% of its threshold, so a value hovering at a threshold does not switch an alert on and off.
- Call the third alert mosquito breeding, and raise it from the trap count alone. Traps cannot detect dengue: an outbreak needs the virus circulating among people, which health authorities establish from reported cases. Microbial counts measure water quality and are no longer part of any alert. A pond water quality alert could use them later.
- Let dengue enter only as a public health notice that an administrator enters to stand in for the health authority. While it is in effect, mosquito checks in that zone open at the watch threshold. The screen says the notice is simulated and that the app does not detect disease.
- Make a simulated scenario the default source of readings, with the live sources one switch away for an administrator, because a presentation cannot wait for real weather. A fixed seed generates the days, so every device and every rehearsal sees the same ones. Wet and dry days come in spells, with an occasional storm. Every member can play the days, step through them, trigger a storm and restart, since each browser holds its own workspace. Changing the data source, entering readings and entering a health notice stay with administrators. Everything the scenario produces is marked as simulated.
- Drive all three simulated readings from one weather series, so the alerts follow each other as they would in the field: forecast, storm, flood, then mosquitoes about a week later. More rain does not simply mean more mosquitoes. A day of very heavy rain flushes larvae out and restarts the count of standing days, after field work in Singapore (Seidahmed and Eltahir 2016, PMC4961380; Benedum et al. 2018, PMC6283346). Those studies are about storm drains. Applying the effect to a whole zone is our assumption.
- Let inspections feed back into the scenario. An asset found healthy lowers its zone's mosquito count for the next seven days. This is a modelling assumption that shows managed green assets staying safe. It is not evidence.
- Keep people out of floodwater. Jobs in a zone open only when its flood warning has ended. A job raised after a flood tells the inspector to report electrical damage and not to touch it. Alerts and checklists ask only for general measures: drain, empty, clear, cover, repair or report.
- The three monitoring locations per zone are illustrative station positions. Active alerts use today's latest readings in the Brussels timezone, or the scenario's current day. Missing data appears as awaiting data. A lower reading clears the map alert while preserving inspection history.
